# Scientific reasoning

Connect component skills to meaningful scientific work and require conclusions to respect the evidence.

**Why:** Correct recall or computation can coexist with a mistaken interpretation. Scientific competence includes understanding what was measured, why a method applies, and what the result supports.

**Apply:**

- Show an early, simplified whole task that explains the goal. Practice needed components, then return to whole tasks with less assistance and greater complexity.
- Connect relevant representations: a mechanism, diagram, equation, table, plot, or code fragment. Ask the learner to explain their relationship instead of presenting disconnected versions.
- For data, teach what an observation represents, its units, how it was obtained, and relevant quality or sampling limits. For a method, practice the assumptions that affect the current conclusion.
- Ask for predictions, competing explanations, method choices, or a justified claim. Choose the scientific dimensions required by the objective; every small section need not cover all of them.
- Make uncertainty part of the response: what remains unknown, what the analysis cannot establish, and what evidence could change the conclusion. Distinguish association from a supported causal claim when relevant.
- Label synthetic data, idealized mechanisms, and simplified algorithms. State which real-world complications are omitted and why that matters. Running supplied code alone does not demonstrate understanding or implementation ability.

**Check:** Can the learner produce a scientifically justified interpretation or action, beyond reproducing a calculation? Does the final whole task combine the capabilities named in the objective?
