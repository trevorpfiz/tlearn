# Attention first

Spend the learner's attention on the topic. Make operating the interface predictable, and give it enough warmth to invite another attempt.

**Why:** Searching for controls, mentally joining separated labels and figures, or tracking unnecessary interface state competes with the intended reasoning. Sparse presentation alone does not make instruction clear.

**Apply:**

- Make the current learning action prominent, with clear reading order and an obvious next action. Use headings and selective emphasis to expose structure rather than surrounding every fragment with a card.
- Put related information together: diagram labels beside their elements, controls beside the affected visual, feedback beside the response. Keep the information permitted by the task available while answering.
- Segment at meaningful steps and let the learner control pacing. Preserve explanations, notation, assumptions, and comparisons needed for the task; do not truncate them to tidy the page.
- Reveal optional hints, solutions, source details, and graph exploration on request. Required information cannot depend on hover. Help disclosure must respect the lesson's retrieval and resource conditions.
- Give an interaction a learning purpose before adding controls or motion. Label values and units, offer reset when useful, and provide pause or step controls for explanatory motion. Static stages may be clearer than animation.
- Design keyboard access, visible focus, readable contrast, responsive layout, and equivalent representations from the beginning. An accessible quiz representation must preserve the task without announcing its solution.
- Express warmth through legible type, modest accents, meaningful illustrations, responsive controls, and task-focused feedback. Avoid competing celebrations, compulsory timers, or progress labels that turn manual completion into mastery.

**Check:** Can the learner find what to do, keep relevant information in view, act, interpret feedback, and continue without learning a new interface? Does each visual distinction or interaction serve understanding, orientation, or accessibility?
